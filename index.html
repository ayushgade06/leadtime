      How it works

      Real seafloor

      16 Terrarium elevation tiles (ETOPO1 and other sources) are decoded in the browser, h = R·256 + G + B/256 − 32768, and averaged to a 512 × 512 grid of Mercator cells about 20 km across. Each row's cell size shrinks with latitude: s = 19.6 km × cos(lat).

      Earthquake to initial wave

      Fault size from magnitude (Strasser et al. 2010): log₁₀L = −2.477 + 0.585·Mw, log₁₀W = −0.882 + 0.351·Mw; slip D = M₀/(μLW) with M₀ = 10^(1.5·Mw + 9.1). The seafloor, and the sea surface with it, rises on the trench side and drops on the land side:

      η₀ = 0.3·D · taper(u, L) · [ G(v − W/4) − 0.6·G(v + W/4) ]

      The 2004 preset uses the 5 published fault segments of Grilli et al. (2007), each breaking at its own time.

      Shallow-water pipes

      Each ocean cell is a water column joined to its 4 neighbours by pipes. A height difference speeds up the flow in a pipe, and the flow moves water between columns:

      dQ/dt = g·h·Δη    dη/dt = (ΣQin − ΣQout) / s²

      Together they give the wave equation with speed c = √(g·h): about 200 m/s (720 km/h) over 4 km of water, slower on shelves, which bends the wave fronts. A positivity limiter, an adaptive time step (dt = 0.4·s/√(g·hmax), about 20 s), a 20-cell absorbing sponge at the edges and shallow-water friction keep it stable. It all runs in a Web Worker.

      Confirmed warning time

      lead = tcity − (tfirst sensor + Tproc)

      A city counts as warned when lead ≥ Tevac. The optimizer adds sensors one at a time, each time choosing the site that warns the most people summed over 15 hypothetical earthquakes.

      Limitations

      • The 20 km grid can't resolve harbours, bays and narrow shelves, so coastal arrival times are approximate (see the 2004 check).
      • A simplified dipole source, not the full Okada (1985) solution, with uniform slip per segment.
      • No inundation or run-up: heights are offshore, not on land.
      • No Coriolis force, wave dispersion or tides.
      • Populations are approximate and mix years and area definitions.

      Why a tsunami crosses an ocean as fast as a jet

      A tsunami moves the whole water column, so its speed depends only on depth: c = √(g·h). Over 4 km of ocean that is about 200 m/s, or 720 km/h. NOAA puts deep-ocean speeds at over 500 mph, "as fast as a jet plane". Tide gauges in 2004 measured a typical 187 m/s.

      Sources: NOAA National Weather Service, Tsunami Safety; Merrifield et al. 2005, GRL.

      How the seafloor steers the wave into beams

      Where the water gets shallower the wave slows down, so a wave front bends toward shallow water, much as light bends in a lens. Ridges and shelves focus energy into beams. In 2004 the strongest beam ran west, straight at Sri Lanka and southern India.

      A warning, then a confirmation

      Warning centres send their first message from earthquake data, within about five minutes where seismic networks are dense. Deep-ocean pressure sensors then confirm whether a tsunami is really coming and how big it is, so the warning can be upgraded or cancelled. That confirmed time is what LeadTime measures. The Indian Ocean had no warning system in 2004; India's centre opened in October 2007 and the regional service started in October 2011.

      Sources: tsunami.gov FAQ; Merrifield et al. 2005; INCOIS; IOC-UNESCO.

      When there is no time: the story of smong

      Close to the earthquake no sensor can help: in our model the wave reaches Banda Aceh about 20 minutes after the shaking. On nearby Simeulue Island, an oral tradition called smong tells people to run to the hills after strong shaking. In 2004 only 7 of its 78,128 people died. Education is the warning system that works in the near field.

      Source: McAdoo, B. G., et al. (2006), Smong: How an oral history saved thousands, Earthquake Spectra 22(S3).
